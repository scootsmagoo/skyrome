# The games in the amphitheatre (AD 113)

Background for `src/arena/` (the scheduled munus in the Flavian Amphitheatre) and the Ludus Magnus routine.

## Trajan's munera
- The Fasti Ostienses (Ostia's official chronicle) record Trajan's second munus: 4,941 pairs of gladiators over 117 days, 4 June 108 to 1 November 109. The Dacian triumph of 107 had already filled the arena for 123 days with 10,000 gladiators (Cassius Dio 68.15).
- A long munus was given again in 113, the year the Column was dedicated (12 May 113). Popular summaries put it at about 2,000 gladiators over some four months.
- In the game, a munus runs for Caesar from 1 May to the eve of the profectio (Trajan left for the East in autumn 113). It rests every fourth day. Great days (Caesar in the pulvinar) are the dedication of the Column, his sixtieth birthday (18 September) and the Kalends of October.

## A day's programme
- **Morning:** the hunts (venationes). There are no animals in the game yet, so the morning has the *prolusio*, pairs fighting with wooden arms (arma lusoria). That is historical as a warm-up, though it was shorter than this.
- **Midday:** the interval. Executions (meridiani) and comic turns were staged then, and much of the house went out for lunch. The sand was raked.
- **Afternoon:** the pompa. A parade of the editor, lictors, trumpeters and the gladiators goes round the sand, and the editor tests the arms (*probatio armorum*). Then come the pairs, one after another, to the cornu, the tuba and the water organ. The star pairs come last.
- **Seating** (lex Iulia theatralis, renewed by Domitian):
  - Senators sit on the podium.
  - Knights and citizens wearing the required toga sit in the maeniana, a sea of white.
  - Women, slaves and the poor sit up in the wooden summum, in colour.
- **Admission** was free, by bone or clay tesserae marked with the entrance, sector and row (the numbered arches survive).
- **Announcements** were painted on walls (*edicta munerum*, as at Pompeii). They gave the number of pairs, the hunts, *sparsiones* (scented sprays) and *vela erunt* (awnings will be up).

## The pairs
- The classic matches were:
  - murmillo v thraex
  - murmillo v hoplomachus
  - secutor v retiarius
  - provocator v provocator
- A referee in a white tunic with clavi and a long staff (the *summa rudis*, with his second, the *secunda rudis*) kept the fight clean and separated the pair.
- A beaten man raised a finger (*ad digitum*). The crowd shouted "Mitte!" (let him go) or "Iugula!" (cut his throat), and the editor decided. Most were spared: trained men were costly, and Augustus banned fights to the death (*sine missione*). Ville estimates about one death in five or six bouts in the first century.
- An even fight could end with both sent off standing (*stantes missi*). Martial (Spect. 29) tells of Priscus and Verus, who were both given the wooden sword of freedom by Titus.
- Stage names were reused across generations:
  - Celadus (thraex) and Crescens (retiarius), from the Pompeii graffiti
  - Astyanax and Kalendio, from the Madrid mosaic
  - Tetraites and Prudes, from the Gaul glass beakers
  - Flamma, from his Sicilian tombstone
  - Spiculus, Nero's favourite
  - Hermes, from Martial 5.24
