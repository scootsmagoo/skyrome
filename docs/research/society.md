# Society, People & Daily Life in Rome, AD 113

Research brief for SKYROME quest, dialogue, NPC, faction and economy designers.
Game window: spring to summer **AD 113** (866 AUC), "the year when Celsus and Crispinus were consuls". Trajan's Forum, Column and Baths are brand new, and Trajan leaves for the Parthian war in autumn.

## How to read this document

Every load-bearing fact carries a confidence tag:

| Tag | Meaning | How designers should use it |
|---|---|---|
| **[A]** | Attested: an ancient source or inscription says it, and scholars broadly agree | Safe to state as fact in dialogue or codex text |
| **[P]** | Probable: scholarly consensus, but the evidence is indirect or the dating is approximate | Safe for gameplay; avoid exact numbers in codex text |
| **[U]** | Uncertain or disputed | Usable, but keep it vague or put it in a character's mouth as opinion or rumor |
| **[G]** | Game extrapolation: plausible, not attested | Free to use. Never present it as history in codex or "Did you know" text |

Short source references use standard abbreviations: Dio = Cassius Dio, *Roman History*; Pan. = Pliny, *Panegyricus*; Ep. = Pliny the Younger, *Letters*; Juv. = Juvenal, *Satires*; Mart. = Martial, *Epigrams*; Suet. = Suetonius; CIL = *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*; Dig. = Justinian's *Digest*. Most texts are on LacusCurtius (penelope.uchicago.edu) or Perseus. A full source list is at the end.

**A general caveat.** Much of what we "know" about daily life comes from Pompeii (before AD 79), from Severan jurists (c. 200–230) or from Diocletian's Price Edict (301). The Edict's prices are inflated and must not be used directly. This document flags any detail borrowed from outside c. 100–120.

**The succession question in 113.** Trajan has no son. Hadrian is the obvious relative: he is married to Trajan's grand-niece and has Plotina's support. Trajan pointedly did not adopt him, and the adoption was claimed only on Trajan's deathbed in 117. Dio (69.1) reports the suspicion that Plotina staged it. The *Historia Augusta* (*Hadr.* 4.8) reports a rumor that Trajan considered the jurist **L. Neratius Priscus**, and another that he meant to leave the choice to the Senate (*Hadr.* 4.9). Both are [U], since the *HA* is unreliable. Senior marshals who could resent Hadrian include **L. Publilius Celsus**, **A. Cornelius Palma** (conqueror of Arabia), **C. Avidius Nigrinus** and **Lusius Quietus**. All four were executed in 118 in the "affair of the four consulars" [A]. Rival patrons, prophecies and wagers on the succession make good quest drivers [G], with real dramatic irony for players who know what happens.

### 1.4 The coming Parthian war

- **Background [A].** Under the settlement of Rhandeia (AD 63), Armenia's king would be a Parthian Arsacid prince, but he had to receive his crown from Rome. Tiridates was crowned by Nero in Rome in 66.
- **The provocation [A].** The Parthian king **Osroes** deposed the Armenian king **Axidares** (Exedares) and installed **Parthamasiris** (also an Arsacid) without Roman consent, c. 110–113.
- **Trajan's response [A].** He mobilised and left Rome in **autumn 113**. The exact day is unattested; some modern accounts say late October [U]. At Athens a Parthian embassy brought gifts and asked him to recognise Parthamasiris. Trajan refused, saying friendship is judged by deeds, not words (Dio 68.17). He reached Antioch around January 114, annexed Armenia in 114 (Parthamasiris was killed), and went on to Mesopotamia and Ctesiphon (115–116).
- **The motive question [A].** Dio (68.17.1) says the real reason was Trajan's **desire for glory**. Modern views add eastern trade (Nabataea had been annexed in 106 and the Via Nova Traiana built) and strategic logic.
- **What Rome feels in summer 113 [G, grounded in P].** Recruiting officers, troops marching out, contractors getting rich, rising prices for horses and mules, war vows on the Capitol, astrologers casting the campaign's horoscope, Parthian envoys or hostages in the city, and Armenian and Syrian traders under suspicion. Senior officers' wives decide whether to follow. This is the natural "main quest drumbeat".
- **Foreshadowing [A].** The great diaspora Jewish revolt (the Kitos War, 115–117), the Antioch earthquake (13 Dec 115, which nearly killed Trajan) and Trajan's death at Selinus (August 117) are all in the future.

### 2.2 Praetorian Guard, Urban Cohorts, Vigiles

These are summarised here; equipment, ranks and pay are in section 9.

- **Praetorians [A/P].** Probably **10 cohorts** in 113 (Domitian had raised the number to 10, and it stayed there until Severus) [P]. They are based at the **Castra Praetoria**. Commanded by two equestrian **praetorian prefects**, they are the emperor's bodyguard. One cohort guarded the Palatine each day and took the watchword from the emperor [A]. They escorted the emperor, made political arrests and carried out executions. They were the best-paid soldiers in the empire and had a reputation for arrogance. Trajan kept them on a tight leash; some of them will march east with him [P].
- **Urban cohorts [P].** Probably **3–4 cohorts** in Rome (numbered X, XI, XII and possibly XIV), each led by a tribune and commanded by the senatorial **praefectus urbi** [U on the count]. They kept day-to-day public order: riots, theatre and circus crowds, collegia disputes. They shared the Castra Praetoria in this period; the separate Castra Urbana came later under Aurelian [P].
- **Vigiles [A/P].** **7 cohorts**, each covering two of the 14 regions of Rome, created by Augustus in AD 6. Cohort size was probably **500–1,000 men**; the date when they were doubled is disputed [U]. Recruits were mostly **freedmen**. Under the Lex Visellia (AD 24) a Junian Latin freedman gained full citizenship after **6 years** of service [A].
  - **Main stations (*stationes*) [P, Platner & Ashby]:**

    | Cohort | Region | Location |
    |---|---|---|
    | I | VII | East side of the Via Lata, opposite the Saepta (shown on the Marble Plan) |
    | II | V | Esquiline, near the south end of modern Piazza Vittorio Emanuele |
    | III | VI | Probably just inside the Porta Viminalis |
    | IV | XII | Aventine, just north of S. Saba |
    | V | II | Caelian, by modern S. Maria in Domnica, just west of the Macellum Magnum |
    | VI | VIII | Location unknown |
    | VII | XIV | Trastevere; main barracks unknown |

    The famous Trastevere *excubitorium* near S. Crisogono is a later conversion of a private house, with graffiti dated 215–245, so **do not place it in 113** [A].
  - **Sub-stations [A].** 14 *excubitoria*, one per region; locations unknown, so place them freely [G].
  - **Fire-fighting kit [A].** *Siphones* (double-action force pumps on carts), *hamae* (buckets of esparto rope sealed with pitch), **ladders**, **hooks** (*unci*) and **axes** (*dolabrae*) for pulling down burning structures, *centones* (rag blankets soaked in water or vinegar to smother flames), mattresses or cushions for people jumping from upper floors, and even **ballistae** to knock down buildings for firebreaks.
  - **Specialists [A, mostly from 2nd–3rd-century inscriptions]:**

    | Specialist | Job |
    |---|---|
    | *Siphonarii* | Pump crews |
    | *Aquarii* | Water supply and bucket lines |
    | *Uncinarii* | Hook-men |
    | *Falciarii* | Men with sickle-hooks |
    | *Centonarii* | Blanket-men |
    | *Ballistarii* | Artillery crews |
    | *Sebaciarii* | Lamp and torch men on night patrol |
    | *Medici* | Doctors |
    | *Quaestionarius* | Interrogator |

  - **Police role [A/P].** They patrolled at night with axes and buckets. They arrested **arsonists, burglars, house-breakers, thieves and fences**, and returned runaway slaves. The prefect of the vigiles judged petty cases himself, could have negligent tenants **beaten** for careless fire-keeping, and sent serious cases to the urban prefect (Dig. 1.15, a Severan source; the principle is older [P]). He could also order every tenant to keep water ready in the apartment (Dig. 1.15.3.4).
  - **Game hook [G].** Night-patrol quests, a fire-fighting mini-game, and inspections that trigger landlord, tenant and bribery choices.

### 2.3 The Senate and the patron–client system

- **The Senate [A].** About 600 members, with a property qualification of **1,000,000 sesterces (HS)**. It met in the **Curia Julia** (restored by Domitian), normally on the **Kalends and Ides**; under Augustus' rule September and October needed only a quorum drawn by lot. In 113 it tried provincial governors for extortion (Pliny prosecuted several), voted honours and approved the emperor's measures. Real power lay with Trajan, but he performed respect for the Senate ("civilitas").
- **Visual markers [A]:**
  - Senators wear a tunic with a **broad purple stripe** (*latus clavus*) and **senatorial shoes** (*calcei*); patricians' shoes bear a crescent (*lunula*).
  - Equestrians (census 400,000 HS) wear a **narrow stripe** and a **gold ring**.
- **Senators and trade [A].** Senators may not engage openly in trade, so they work through freedmen agents. That makes a classic "quest-giver behind a front man" [G].
- **Patron and client [A]:**
  - At dawn, clients in togas queue for the ***salutatio*** at the patron's door.
  - They receive the ***sportula***: in Martial and Juvenal's time a cash dole of **100 *quadrantes*** (6¼ HS) a day.
  - They escort the patron through the Forum and applaud his speeches and *recitationes*.
  - In return the patron gives legal help, recommendations, dinner invitations (sometimes humiliating ones: Juvenal 5 has the poor client served worse food than the host) and loans.
  - Freed slaves owe their former masters *obsequium* (respect) and *operae* (days of work).
- **Game system [G].** A **Patronage** track. Each patron gives access to a set of quests and favours, and asks for daily *salutatio* attendance (a time cost). Abandoning a patron costs reputation. The player can later acquire clients of their own.

### 2.4 Collegia (guilds and associations)

- **Legal status [A].** Associations needed Senate or emperor approval (the Julian law on collegia). Trajan himself refused to let Nicomedia form a fire brigade of 150 men, because whatever they are called, such groups turn into political clubs (Pliny Ep. 10.33–34). Burial clubs of the poor (*collegia tenuiorum*) were generally allowed to meet monthly and collect dues.
- **Burial-club mechanics [A, but from AD 136].** The Lanuvium inscription of 136 (the cult of Diana and Antinous) records an entry fee of **100 HS plus an amphora of good wine**, dues of **5 *asses* a month**, and a **funeral grant of 300 HS**. It also sets fines for brawling at dinners. A great template for guild mechanics.

### 2.5 Priests and Vestals

- **Vestal Virgins [A]:**
  - Six priestesses serving for 30 years, led by the *Virgo Vestalis Maxima*, living in the **Atrium Vestae** in the Forum.
  - They tend the eternal fire. If it went out, the Vestal responsible was scourged by the pontifex maximus.
  - A Vestal found unchaste was **buried alive**. Domitian had the chief Vestal Cornelia executed this way c. 91 (Pliny Ep. 4.11), so it is within living memory.
  - Vestals had a lictor and the right to make wills.
  - If a condemned man on his way to execution met a Vestal by chance, he was spared (Plutarch, *Numa* 10) [A]. A ready-made quest.
  - The names of the Vestals in 113 are unknown [U/G].
- **Pontifices [A].** About 16, with Trajan as **pontifex maximus**. They oversee the calendar, sacred law, burials and adoptions. The *rex sacrorum* and the **flamines** (the *flamen Dialis*, hedged with taboos: he may not touch a horse, look at an army or wear a knot) are also under the pontifical college.
- **Augurs [A].** About 16. They take the auspices from bird signs and the feeding of sacred chickens (*pulli*; eager eating, *tripudium*, is a good sign). Pliny the Younger became an augur c. 103 in Frontinus' place.
- ***Quindecimviri sacris faciundis* [A].** They keep and consult the **Sibylline Books**, held in the Temple of Apollo on the Palatine, and oversee foreign cults such as Magna Mater. Tacitus was one.
- ***Septemviri epulones* [A].** Organise the sacred banquets, for example the *epulum Iovis* on 13 Sep.
- **Fetiales [A].** Twenty priests of treaties and war. In the old ritual a fetial threw a spear at the "Column of War" in front of the Temple of Bellona to declare war; it is attested for Octavian in 32 BC and Marcus Aurelius in 178. **Its use for Trajan's Parthian war is not attested** [U], but it would be a spectacular set-piece [G].
- **Haruspices [A].** Etruscan-style entrail diviners; Claudius organised an *ordo* of 60.
- **Arval Brothers [A].** Twelve priests of Dea Dia with a grove at the 5th milestone of the Via Campana. Their May festival is moveable. They made vows for the emperor's safety and return; their inscribed records cover Trajan's departures for Dacia in 101 and 105, so vows for the Parthian expedition are likely [P].
- **Salii [A].** Twenty-four young patricians who dance through the streets in March and October with archaic shields (*ancilia*). October 19 is the *Armilustrium*.
- **Imperial cult [A].** Sodalities for the deified Augustus, Vespasian and Titus, and new priests for **Diva Marciana** in 113.

### 2.6 Gladiatorial ludi and circus factions

Full details are in section 10 and section 3.

- **Imperial training schools [A]**, clustered east of the Amphitheatre:
  - **Ludus Magnus**: built by Domitian, rebuilt under Trajan, with its own practice arena.
  - **Ludus Dacicus**.
  - **Ludus Gallicus**.
  - **Ludus Matutinus**: for the "morning" beast-fighters and hunters.
  - Each was run by an equestrian **procurator**.
  - The neighbouring buildings are the *Armamentarium* (arms store), *Spoliarium* (where the dead were stripped), *Saniarium* (infirmary), *Summum Choragium* (stage machinery) and the **Castra Misenatium** (sailors who work the awnings).
- **Private *lanistae* [A].** Dealers-trainers who own or hire out gladiators. They are legally *infames* (disgraced), rich and despised.
- **Circus factions [A]:**
  - The four colors: **Russata** (Reds), **Albata** (Whites), **Prasina** (Greens) and **Veneta** (Blues).
  - Domitian's Purple and Gold factions were dropped after his death.
  - Each faction is a business run by a *dominus factionis* (often an equestrian), with stables (*stabula*) in the **Campus Martius**, slave and freedman charioteers, grooms, vets and spies.
  - Trajan **enlarged the Circus Maximus** by about 5,000 places (Pan. 51) [A].
  - Pliny sneered that fans "love a piece of cloth" (*favent panno, pannum amant*, Ep. 9.6) [A].
  - The great charioteer **Scorpus** died c. 95. **Diocles** made his debut in **122**, so he is not active in 113 [A]. Invent the current stars [G].

### 2.7 The grain supply and the dole

- **Free grain [A].** About **200,000** adult male citizens resident in Rome are registered for **5 *modii* of grain a month** (about 33 kg, more than one man needs). It is distributed by token (*tessera*) at the **Porticus Minucia Frumentaria** in the Campus Martius; the location is disputed, perhaps near the modern Via delle Botteghe Oscure [U]. Each recipient has a numbered doorway (*ostium*) and a fixed day.
- **Trajan's additions [A].** He added about **5,000 freeborn children** to the lists (Pan. 26–28).
- **Gifts of cash [A/P].** Trajan gave three ***congiaria*** (cash gifts to the people), traditionally totalling 650 *denarii* per head [P]. His ***alimenta*** schemes fund poor children in Italian towns [A].
- **Prefect of the grain supply [A].** An equestrian official responsible for the Egyptian and African grain fleets, the warehouses (*horrea*: Galbana, Agrippiana and others) and Ostia–Portus, where **Trajan's hexagonal harbor basin** was built c. 100–112 [P].
- **Anachronism [A].** Free **bread**, **pork** and **oil** handouts are later (Septimius Severus and Aurelian). In 113 it is grain only.
- **Game hooks [G].** Token forgery, grain theft from barges, hoarding speculators in a lean month, a riot at a distribution door, and escorting a grain convoy from Ostia.

### 2.10 Astrologers and magicians

- **Astrologers [A].** *Mathematici* or "Chaldaeans" were expelled from Rome repeatedly (16, 52, 69–70, under Domitian) but always came back. **No expulsion is known under Trajan.** Casting the **emperor's** horoscope or asking about his death is treason. Elite women consulting astrologers is a satirical staple (Juvenal 6, published a little later).
- **Magic [A].** The Cornelian law on murderers and poisoners covered poison and harmful magic; the same word, *veneficium*, means both. The Twelve Tables already forbade "singing away" a neighbour's crops.
- **Game hook [G].** An astrologer's booth near the Circus Maximus, where professional diviners plied for trade [P]; a black-market horoscope of Trajan as a treason quest.

### 2.11 Foreign communities

- **Jews [A]:**
  - The main community is in **Trastevere** (Philo, *Legatio* 155), with others near the **Porta Capena**. Juvenal 3.12–16 describes the grove of Egeria rented out to Jews "whose furniture is a basket and hay".
  - The bully in Juvenal 3.296 sneers "in which prayer-house (*proseucha*) shall I look for you?"
  - Since 70 every Jew paid the ***fiscus Iudaicus*** (2 *denarii* a year). Nerva curbed the abuses of informers in collecting it (his coins read FISCI IVDAICI CALVMNIA SVBLATA).
  - Synagogue congregations are later named in catacomb inscriptions (Augustesians, Agrippesians, Volumnesians, Suburesians and others); several names are probably early [P].
  - The population estimate of 20,000–50,000 is [U].
  - The Jewish catacombs mostly date from the later 2nd–4th centuries [P].
- **Egyptians and Isis [A].** The **Iseum Campense** in the Campus Martius was rebuilt by Domitian. Priests have shaven heads and wear linen; worshippers shake the **sistrum** and use Nile water; there are daily morning and afternoon services. Martial 10.48 says the temple crowd "announces the eighth hour". The big Serapeum on the Quirinal is **Caracallan**, so it is an anachronism in 113.
- **Syrians [A].** "Long ago the Syrian Orontes flowed into the Tiber" (Juv. 3.62). They are merchants, musicians, *ambubaiae* (Syrian flute-girls) and cult personnel. Their sanctuaries on the Janiculum and Aventine, including Jupiter Dolichenus, are mostly later 2nd–3rd century [P], so keep them small or informal in 113.
- **Greeks [A].** Everywhere: doctors, teachers, artists, secretaries, slaves and freedmen. Juvenal's Umbricius "cannot bear a Greek city, citizens" (3.60–61) and mocks the hungry little Greek (*Graeculus esuriens*, 3.78) who will be anything you want.
- **Others [P/G].** **Dacian captives** (slaves and gladiators since 106), Gauls, Spaniards (Trajan's countrymen enjoy prestige), North Africans (Lusius Quietus' Moors), Germans and Britons, envoys from **India** (present at Trajan's 107 games, Dio 68.15) [A], Nabataean Arabs, Armenians and Parthians (suspect in 113 [G]).
- **Christians [A].** A small community meeting in houses. Pliny's letter to Trajan (Ep. 10.96–97, c. 112) sets the policy: Christians are **not to be hunted out** and **anonymous accusations are not admitted**, but those who are accused and refuse to sacrifice are punished. Ignatius of Antioch was thrown to the beasts in Rome "under Trajan" by tradition, c. 107–117 [U on the date]. Christian **catacombs as such are later (c. 200 onward)**.

### 2.12 Mystery cults

- **Mithras: what the evidence shows c. 113 [A/P]:**
  1. **Statius**, *Thebaid* 1.719–20 (written c. 80–92), mentions a god "beneath the rocks of a Persian cave" twisting the horns of a bull, Mithras. It is the earliest literary echo of the bull-slaying scene in Rome.
  2. **CIL VI 718** (CIMRM 593/594), from Rome, is a bull-slaying dedication to Sol Mithras by **Alcimus, slave steward of Ti. Claudius Livianus**, Trajan's praetorian prefect. It dates to roughly 101–120 and is among the very earliest Mithraic monuments anywhere.
  3. **No Mithraeum in Rome is securely dated as early as 113**; the known ones (Santa Prisca, Barberini, Circus Maximus) are later [P].

  **Recommendation.** Mithras in 113 is a **new, small, secretive cult** spreading among soldiers, imperial slaves and freedmen, with a link to the praetorian prefect's household. That is perfect for a hidden "guild" quest line [G]. Use the seven grades (Raven, Bride, Soldier, Lion, Persian, Sun-runner, Father) with caution: they are attested later [P].
- **Isis [A].** Initiation rites, the *Navigium Isidis* (5 March) and the *Isia* or "finding of Osiris" in late October to early November. The festival dates come from later calendars [P].
- **Magna Mater (Cybele) [A]:**
  - Temple on the Palatine; festival of the **Megalesia** (4–10 April).
  - Her eunuch **Galli** are foreign priests in bright robes with tambourines, cymbals and begging bowls. Roman citizens were not allowed to become Galli, and Domitian banned castration (Suet. *Dom.* 7). How Galli were recruited legally in 113 is unclear [U].
  - The high priest (*archigallus*) and the bull-blood rite (*taurobolium*) are **later** (mid-2nd century on) [P].
- **Bacchus [A].** The Senate's decree of 186 BC suppressing the Bacchanalia is still remembered. Private Bacchic associations of wealthy households existed; the Torre Nova inscription is c. 160s [P]. Liber Pater is worshipped publicly at the Liberalia (17 March).
- **Game hooks [G].** An initiation questline for each cult (Mithras, Isis, Bacchus), each giving small perks, secret contacts and a moral wrinkle. Keep the "revelations" ambiguous.

## 3. Daily life

### 3.1 Time: the Roman day

- **Hours [A].** Daylight is divided into **12 *horae*** from sunrise to sunset, so hour length varies with the season. Night has 12 night-hours, and the military and the vigiles divide it into **4 watches (*vigiliae*)**.
- **Hour lengths in Rome (41.9°N) [P, computed]:**

  | Date | Daylight | One *hora* |
  |---|---|---|
  | Late April | about 13 h 40 m | about 68 min |
  | June solstice | about 15 h 10 m | about 76 min |
  | Late August | about 13 h 30 m | about 68 min |
  | September equinox | about 12 h 10 m | about 61 min |
  | Mid October | about 11 h | about 55 min |

  **Engine rule [G]:** hour *n* begins at `sunrise + (n-1) * daylight/12`. The sixth hour ends at local solar noon.
- **Martial's day [A].** Martial 4.8 gives the Roman routine:
  - Hours 1–2: the *salutatio*.
  - Hour 3: lawyers are hoarse in the courts.
  - Until hour 5: the city does its various jobs.
  - Hour 6: rest.
  - Hour 7: the end of work.
  - Hours 8–9: exercise and the baths.
  - Hour 9: dinner couches.

  Martial 10.48: at the **8th hour** the Isis crowd announces the time, the praetorian guard changes and the baths are at the right temperature [P].
- **Telling time [A].** Sundials (including the Augustan Horologium in the Campus Martius) and water-clocks. Rich men keep a slave to call out the hours.
- **Game NPC schedule template [G]:**

  | Time | Activity |
  |---|---|
  | Dawn | *Salutatio* |
  | Hours 2–6 | Work, courts, markets |
  | Hour 6 | *Prandium* (lunch) and siesta |
  | Hours 8–9 | Exercise and baths |
  | Hours 9–10 | *Cena* (dinner) |
  | After dark | Delivery carts, taverns, patrols, crime |

### 3.2 Baths

- **Entry fee [A].** A ***quadrans*** (¼ *as*) at ordinary baths (Horace, Seneca, Juvenal); children enter free. In the Hadrianic mining town of Vipasca in Portugal, men paid ½ *as* and women 1 *as* [A, outside Rome].
- **Opening hours [P].** Usually from about the 7th–8th hour (around midday) to dusk. Some had separate hours or wings for women. At Vipasca women bathed from dawn to the 7th hour and men from the 8th hour to the 2nd hour of the night [A, outside Rome]. Hadrian later **banned mixed bathing** (*HA Hadr.* 18), which implies it happened in 113 [P].
- **Kinds of bath [A].** Imperial ***thermae*** (Agrippa, Nero, Titus and the new **Baths of Trajan**, dedicated 22 June 109) and hundreds of small private *balneae*.
- **Sequence [A].** Undress (*apodyterium*), exercise (*palaestra*: ball games, weights, wrestling), warm room (*tepidarium*), hot room (*caldarium*), cold plunge (*frigidarium*), and oiling and scraping with a *strigil*. Libraries, snack sellers, hair-pluckers and masseurs were part of the experience.
- **Soundscape [A].** Seneca, *Ep.* 56, living above a bath, lists the grunts of weightlifters, the slap of the masseur, the ball-player counting his score, the hair-plucker's shrill cries, a man who loves his own voice in the bath, and the sausage, cake and drink sellers each with their own call. Ready-made ambient audio.

### 3.3 Meals

- ***Ientaculum* (breakfast, at dawn) [A].** Bread dipped in wine or with salt, cheese, olives, honey; often skipped.
- ***Prandium* (around the 6th hour) [A].** A light, often cold meal: leftovers, bread, fish or eggs.
- ***Cena* (the 9th or 10th hour, the main meal) [A].** For the elite: *gustatio* (eggs, olives, vegetables), *prima mensa* (meat and fish) and *secunda mensa* (fruit and pastries), "from egg to apples" (*ab ovo usque ad mala*). Diners recline three to a couch with the *triclinium* arrangement. *Garum* (fish sauce) is everywhere.
- **Poor and street food [A/P].** *Puls* (porridge), bread, chickpeas and lupins sold hot, sausages, cheap wine, *posca* (vinegar and water). Most **insula** flats had no kitchen, so the poor ate at ***popinae*** and ***cauponae*** (taverns and inns) or bought from street stalls. "Thermopolium" is Plautine and modern usage; in 113 say *popina* [P].

### 3.5 Night, noise and danger

- **Daytime cart ban [A].** Wheeled traffic was banned from sunrise to the 10th hour (Julian municipal law), except for public and temple construction, refuse removal and processions. So **deliveries and building materials move at night**.
- **Juvenal Satire 3 as a design doc [A]:**
  - **3.232–8:** insomnia kills the sick; carts creak through narrow bends and the drovers swear.
  - **3.243–8:** in the daytime crowd you are elbowed and hit by a beam or a wine-cask.
  - **3.254–61:** a wagon carrying a huge Ligurian marble block overturns on the crowd and "nothing is left of the bodies". Very on-theme for Trajan's building boom.
  - **3.268–77:** at night pots fall from the windows, so "make your will before going out to dinner".
  - **3.278–301:** the drunken bully who picks a fight: "Where are you from? Whose sour wine and beans are you full of?"
  - **3.302–8:** burglars and armed robbers.
- **Martial's street noise [A].** Martial 12.57 lists schoolmasters shouting before dawn, bakers at night, coppersmiths hammering, money-changers rattling coins on their tables, Bellona's frenzied devotees, a shipwrecked sailor begging, a Jewish boy taught by his mother to beg, and a hawker of sulphur matches.
- **Lighting [A].** Streets are unlit. The rich walk with slaves carrying torches or lanterns; the poor walk in the dark.
- **Game hook [G].** A night-safety system: higher mugging and accident chances in unlit districts; hire a torch-bearer; "falling pot" hazards near insulae.

### 3.6 Housing, fire and collapse

- **Insulae [A].** Apartment blocks of several floors. Augustus capped heights at 70 Roman feet; **Trajan lowered the cap to 60 feet** (about 17.7 m), "because of collapses" (*Epitome de Caesaribus* 13). Ground floors held shops (*tabernae*) and the best apartments; the higher the floor, the poorer and more dangerous the room.
- **Fire [A].** Rome burned often: the great fire of 64, the fire of 80 on the Capitol and Campus Martius, a fire of 104 that damaged the Golden House (so the Baths of Trajan were built over part of it), and the Pantheon struck by lightning and burned in 110. Juvenal 3.197–202: the fire spreads upward and the poor man under the roof tiles is last to know. Summer is the worst season [P].
- **Collapse [A].** Juvenal 3.190–6: landlords prop up cracked walls and say "sleep easy". Cicero joked that even the mice had moved out of his collapsing shops (*Att.* 14.9).
- **Rent [A].** Juvenal 3.223–5: a year's rent for a dark garret in Rome would buy a house outright in a small Latin town.
- **Game hooks [G].** Landlord quests, rent extortion, a collapse event, and arson-for-insurance plots (Romans had no insurance, so instead arson to clear a site or cover a theft).

### 3.7 Tiber floods, water and sanitation

- **Tiber floods [A].** Floods mostly come in winter and spring. Pliny Ep. 8.17 (c. 107) describes a severe flood despite the relief channel "which our most provident emperor made". Trajan's reign is when the curators of the Tiber channel and banks first also take charge of the **city sewers** [P].
- **Aqueducts in 113 [A].** Ten: Appia, Anio Vetus, Marcia, Tepula, Julia, Virgo, Alsietina, Claudia, Anio Novus, and **Aqua Traiana** (24 June 109). The **curator of the water supply** is the official; Frontinus wrote his manual c. 97–98.
  - Frontinus counts hundreds of public basins (*lacus*, about 591), display fountains and reservoirs [P on the exact counts].
  - **Water theft** by illegal taps and bribed water-men is a known fraud (Frontinus). The 9 BC law on aqueducts fined damage at 100,000 HS [A]. A quest hook.
- **Sanitation [A].** The Cloaca Maxima and a sewer network; public multi-seat **latrines** (*foricae*); a sponge-on-a-stick (*xylospongium*, though its use is debated). Fullers collected urine in jars, and Vespasian taxed it ("*non olet*", it doesn't smell). Chamber pots were emptied into vats under stairs or, illegally, out of windows. The dead were buried **outside** the city boundary.
- **Disease [A].** Malaria ("fevers", tertian and quartan) peaks in **late summer and autumn**; the rich leave for their villas, and Horace dreads the August fig-season fevers. A **"Fever season" debuff** from August to October is grounded and atmospheric [G].

### 4.5 Recommended game currency scheme [G]

1. **Store all money internally as an integer number of *quadrantes*** (1 HS = 16 q). The bath fee (1 q) and a Falernian cup (16 q) are then exact. Luxury sums such as 100,000 HS are only 1.6 million q, which is safe for JS numbers.
2. **Show players the sestertius (HS) as the main unit**, the way Romans priced things, with small change in *asses*: "12 HS 3 *as*". Offer an option to show *denarii* and *aurei*. Coins in loot are named by type ("a worn *dupondius* of Domitian").
3. **Scale.** 1 HS is roughly one Skyrim gold coin, and the curve then feels familiar:
   - food: fractions of an HS;
   - weapons: 50–150 HS;
   - armor: hundreds;
   - a horse: about 1,500;
   - a slave: about 2,000;
   - a small house in a Latin town: 10–30k;
   - a house in Rome: 100k to millions.
4. **Optional realism: coin weight.** 1,000 HS in sesterces weighs about 25 kg; in *aurei* about 73 g. Encumbrance pushes players toward gold, bankers and **letters of credit** (*permutatio*) for long trips.
5. **Money sinks.** Patron gifts, collegium dues, temple vows, rent, bribes to aediles and vigiles, the *sportula* you must hand out once you have clients, betting, and legal fees. Advocates' fees were capped at 10,000 HS by Claudius, and under Trajan litigants had to swear they had paid nothing in advance (Pliny Ep. 5.9).

## 5. Calendar and festivals, April to October 113

Dates are Julian. Roman dates are counted back from the Kalends (1st), the Nones (5th, but the 7th in March, May, July and October) and the Ides (13th, but the 15th in those four months).

- **Unlucky days [A].** The day after each Kalends, Nones and Ides is a *dies ater*. **May** is unlucky for weddings, as is **June until the 15th**.
- **Ludi [A].** Public games (*ludi*) are mostly theatre days followed by circus races at the end. Gladiatorial **munera** are separate, usually imperial or private gifts, not fixed festivals.

## 6. Religion and the "grounded supernatural"

Design principle [G]: **nothing on screen is unambiguously magical.** Every "supernatural" mechanic should have a natural explanation available: coincidence, psychology, fraud, medicine or crime. Rome is full of belief, and the game rewards engaging with that belief, without confirming it.

| Belief / practice | Historical core | Low-fantasy mechanic [G] |
|---|---|---|
| **Lares, Penates, Genius** | Household gods in the *lararium* receive offerings on the Kalends, Nones and Ides [A]. A man's *genius*, a woman's *iuno*. **Crossroads shrines** (Lares Compitales and the Genius of Augustus) are run by neighbourhood officials (*vicomagistri*), often freedmen; Rome had 265 neighbourhoods in AD 73 (Pliny, *NH* 3.66) [A]. | Home-base shrine: daily offering, small "well-rested" or morale buff. Neighbourhood shrines are hubs for local reputation and quests. |
| **Oaths** | Swearing by Jupiter, or **by the Genius of the emperor** (a serious oath; perjury by it was punishable) [A] | An NPC's oath is binding; break your own and pious NPCs react badly |
| **Vows (*vota*)** | A contract with a god: "*do ut des*" (I give so that you may give). You vow an offering if helped, then pay it. "V.S.L.M." (*votum solvit libens merito*, he paid his vow willingly, as deserved) is on thousands of altars [A]. | **Vow system**: pledge X to a god before a hard quest. On success the debt opens; an unpaid vow brings rising ill omens and pious NPCs' disapproval. |
| **Omens** | Sneezes, stumbling at the threshold, overheard chance words, birds, lightning. Crucially, **an omen could be accepted or refused by a verbal formula** ("*omen accipio*" / "*absit omen*"), so omens were in one's own power (Pliny, *NH* 28.17) [A]. | Random omen events with an **accept/refuse** choice. Accepting a good omen gives a short confidence buff; refusing a bad one costs a small action (re-cross the threshold, spit, touch an amulet). The underlying effects are pure RNG and morale. |
| **Augury** | Augurs read birds and sacred chickens; generals took the auspices before battle [A] | Pay an augur before a big undertaking. Favourable signs raise companions' morale (they fight better), but nothing alters the world. A cynic NPC can rig the chickens, as Roman generals were accused of doing. |
| **Haruspicy** | Reading entrails (the liver especially) and lightning; the Etruscan *ordo* of 60 [A] | Pre-quest "reading" gives a vague hint that is sometimes actually useful intel, because the haruspex is well informed [G] |
| **Prodigies** | Sweating statues, monstrous births, rains of blood, lightning on temples; expiated by public rites [A] | World events that shift the city mood (prices, riots, temple traffic) and spawn investigation quests, often with mundane causes |
| **Lightning** | Struck places were walled off and the lightning "buried" (*bidental*, *fulgur conditum*) [A] | Taboo spots; desecration has reputation consequences |
| **Curse tablets (*defixiones*)** | Lead sheets inscribed "I bind X, his tongue, hands, feet...", rolled, pierced with a nail and deposited in graves, wells or springs. Common against charioteers, rivals in love and lawsuits, and thieves. Rome's **Anna Perenna fountain** produced many, mostly later. Commissioned from *magi* or written yourself [A]. | Commission or find curse tablets. Effects: the target NPC, if told, becomes fearful (morale or performance penalty), and a superstitious charioteer may actually lose. Thieves can be **scared into returning goods** by a public curse. Effects never exceed the plausible. |
| **Amulets** | ***Bulla*** (freeborn boys until manhood, around 15–16), ***lunula*** (girls), ***fascinum*** (phallic charms against the **evil eye**, *invidia*), phallic wind-chimes (*tintinnabula*) [A] | An "evil eye protection" slot: lowers the frequency of bad-omen events (RNG only), and superstitious NPCs trust you more. |
| ***Strix*** **and witches** | The *strix* is a screech-owl-like night creature that sucks infants' blood; the goddess Carna drives it off with **whitethorn** (Ovid, *Fasti* 6.131ff) [A]. Witches (*sagae*, *veneficae*) such as Horace's **Canidia** gather bones and herbs in the old Esquiline graveyard (Hor. *Sat.* 1.8) [A]. | Infant-death quest: the "strix" is disease, poison or a human killer. Whitethorn branches as a calming quest item. A "witch" NPC who is really a herbalist or poisoner. |
| **Werewolves, ghost stories** | Petronius 61–63: a freedman tells of a soldier who strips at a graveyard and becomes a wolf (*versipellis*); a story of *strigae* stealing a dead boy [A] | Campfire and tavern stories for ambient dialogue; a "werewolf" bandit in a wolfskin. |
| ***Lemures* / *larvae*** | Restless, malign dead, appeased at the **Lemuria**. ### 7.3 Terms of address [A]

- ***Domine*** / ***Domina***: "sir/madam", literally "master/mistress". By now used politely even between equals (Martial, Seneca).
- ***Mi frater***, ***soror***, ***pater***, ***mater***: also used affectionately or deferentially to non-relatives.
- ***Puer***: "boy", used to call a slave of any age. ***Ancilla***: maid.
- ***Amice*** (friend), ***hospes*** (stranger, guest or host), ***Quirites*** (citizens, when addressing a crowd), ***Patres conscripti*** (Senators).
- The emperor: ***Caesar***, ***Imperator***, ***Princeps***, informally *optime princeps*. ***Dominus et deus*** was Domitian's style and Trajan rejected it; using it is a **faux pas or a sign of a sycophant** [A].
- Soldiers: *commilito* (fellow soldier); *miles*.

### 7.4 Naming conventions [A]

- **Citizen men (*tria nomina*):** *praenomen* + *nomen* (family) + *cognomen*, often with filiation and voting tribe: *M. Ulpius M. f. Pap. Traianus*. Romans of 113 mostly use the cognomen ("Traianus", "Plinius Secundus" as "Secundus").
- **Common praenomina:** Gaius (C.), Lucius (L.), Marcus (M.), Publius (P.), Quintus (Q.), Titus (T.), Tiberius (Ti.), Gnaeus (Cn.), Sextus (Sex.), Aulus (A.), Decimus (D.), Servius (Ser.), Spurius (Sp.), Manius (M'.).
- **Common nomina:** Aelius, Aemilius, Annius, Antonius, Aurelius, Caecilius, Calpurnius, Claudius, Cornelius, Fabius, Flavius, Fulvius, Julius, Junius, Licinius, Marius, Octavius, Pompeius, Sempronius, Sulpicius, Terentius, Ulpius, Valerius, Vibius.
- **Common cognomina:** Rufus, Niger, Longus, Crassus, Celer, Maximus, Priscus, Severus, Sabinus, Fronto, Naso, Varus, Proculus, Pollio, Secundus, Celsus, Clemens, Fuscus.
- **Women:** the feminine of the family nomen (*Ulpia*, *Cornelia*, *Julia*), often plus a cognomen (*Plotina*, *Marciana*, *Sabina*, *Faustina*, *Rufina*, *Procula*, *Tertulla*, *Marcella*). Sisters are distinguished as *Maior*/*Minor* or *Prima*/*Secunda*/*Tertia*. **A married woman keeps her own family name.**
- **Slaves:** a single name, often Greek (Eros, Hermes, Onesimus, Epaphroditus, Philargyrus, Chrysis, Helpis, Daphne, Irene, Thallusa, Chloe), "lucky" Latin (Felix, Fortunatus, Faustus, Hilarus, Successus, Primus, Prima), or ethnic (Syrus, Afer, Dacus, Gallus). They are identified by their master: *Alcimus Ti. Claudi Liviani servus* ("Alcimus, slave of Ti. Claudius Livianus").
- **Freedmen:** take the patron's praenomen and nomen, with their slave name as cognomen. A slave Felix freed by M. Ulpius Traianus becomes **M. Ulpius Felix**, written *M. Ulpius Aug(usti) lib(ertus) Felix*. Earlier imperial freedmen are *Ti. Claudii* and *T. Flavii*. **A Greek cognomen often signals servile descent** (social snobbery fuel).
- **New citizens:** provincials enfranchised by Trajan, such as auxiliary veterans, become ***M. Ulpii***.
- **Non-citizens (*peregrini*):** a single name plus father's name ("Demetrius son of Apollonius"); Semitic names among Syrians, Jews and Nabataeans.

## 8. Crime and law

### 8.1 Status and punishment in 113

- **Terminology [P].** The formal legal distinction between ***honestiores*** (the "more honourable": senators, equestrians, local councillors, veterans) and ***humiliores*** (everyone else) appears in jurists of the **later 2nd–3rd century** (see Garnsey, *Social Status and Legal Privilege*, 1970). In 113 it is **emerging in practice but not yet systematic**.
- **What already applies [A]:**
  - **Citizens versus non-citizens.** Citizens could appeal, the right of *provocatio* (Paul appealed to Caesar), and were traditionally spared crucifixion and flogging with rods.
  - **Slaves** could be tortured for evidence, crucified, burned or thrown to beasts.
  - **Elites** facing a capital charge were usually allowed exile or suicide.
  - **The Silanian decree (AD 10)**: if a master is murdered at home, **all slaves under that roof** are interrogated and executed. In AD 61, 400 slaves of Pedanius Secundus were executed (Tac. *Ann.* 14.42–5). A brutal quest premise.

| Group (113) | Typical penalties |
|---|---|
| Senators, equestrians | Trial by the Senate or the emperor; **relegation** (banishment, keeping property) or **deportation** to an island (loss of citizenship and property); loss of rank; confiscation; occasionally beheading by sword, or invited suicide |
| Free poor citizens | Fines; beating; **condemnation to the mines** (*ad metalla*, a form of penal slavery), **to a gladiatorial school** (*ad ludum*) or to public works; beheading for serious crimes, and increasingly the harsher forms over the 2nd century |
| Non-citizens | Whatever the magistrate or governor decides, including **beasts**, the **cross** and **burning** |
| Slaves | Whipping, chains, sale abroad, the slave-prison (*ergastulum*), mines, crucifixion, beasts, burning (arson, or killing a master) |
| Soldiers | Military discipline: the vine-stick, demotion, the cudgel (*fustuarium*), dishonourable discharge |

**Penal labor [A].** Pliny asked Trajan what to do with condemned men working as public slaves in Bithynia. Trajan ruled that those condemned in the last 10 years should go back to their sentence, and older convicts could clean **baths and sewers** and do road work (Ep. 10.31–32). A model for penal-labor NPCs.

### 8.3 Process

- **Civil cases [A].** The plaintiff summons the defendant himself (*in ius vocatio*) and may **drag him to court** if he refuses, after calling a bystander as witness (Horace *Sat.* 1.9 ends with exactly this). The praetor sets out the formula and appoints a private judge (*iudex*).
- **Criminal cases [A].**
  - There is no public prosecutor. A **private accuser** files a charge and risks penalties for **malicious prosecution** (*calumnia*); the old story says false accusers were branded with a K [U].
  - Magistrates increasingly investigate directly (*cognitio extra ordinem*) [P].
  - Slaves' evidence is taken **under torture**.
  - Advocates (*patroni*) speak, timed by water-clock.
- **Custody [A].** Rome had **no prison sentences for free people**: "prison is for holding men, not punishing them" (Ulpian, later). Options:
  - the ***Carcer*** (with the Tullianum below) at the foot of the Capitol, for holding prisoners and executing state enemies;
  - the ***Lautumiae*** quarry-prison [P];
  - **"military custody"**: chained by the wrist to a soldier, as Paul was in Rome (Acts 28) [A]. A great escort or escape mechanic.
  - **open custody** in a magistrate's house;
  - private ***ergastula*** (prisons for slaves).
- **Sanctuary [P].** At the emperor's statue for slaves; meeting a Vestal (section 2.5).
- **The Gemonian Stairs [A].** Bodies of the executed are displayed on the stairs by the Carcer and then thrown into the Tiber. Decebalus' head was brought to Rome in 106 (Dio 68.14); modern accounts say it was thrown down the Stairs [U].

### 8.4 Plausible "bounty" mechanics [G, grounded in A]

1. **Notice board.** A crier (*praeco*) announces in the Forum, or a notice is painted on a wall: lost property or a runaway slave, with a cash reward. Petronius 97: a crier offers **1,000 HS** for a lost slave boy. Pompeii CIL IV 64 offers **65 HS** for a stolen bronze pot plus more for the thief.
2. **Slave-catcher (*fugitivarius*) contracts.** Return a runaway for a fee, or help them escape. A morally loaded quest line.
3. **Vigiles and urban cohort "wanted" lists.** Arsonists, burglars and bandits. Rewards come from the prefect's office or from victims [G].
4. **Informing (*delatio*).** Report tax evasion, hidden property or (very risky) treason. In 113 informers are hated and **Trajan punishes malicious accusers**, so each use carries a reputation hit with most factions and a chance of a *calumnia* backlash.
5. **Private vengeance.** Hire muscle, write a curse tablet, or bring a private prosecution with its costs and risks.
6. **Road bandits (*latrones*).** Bounties from landowners and municipal officials once the game expands into Latium.

## 10. Gladiators and venationes

### 10.1 Who fights and why [A]

- **Recruits:** slaves bought by a *lanista*; **prisoners of war** (many Dacians after 106); **criminals condemned to a ludus**, who could survive and earn release; and **free volunteers** (*auctorati*). A volunteer swore the oath to be "burned, bound, beaten and killed by the sword" (*uri, vinciri, verberari, ferroque necari*, Petronius 117; Seneca *Ep.* 37).
- **Status:** gladiators are legally ***infames*** yet are celebrity heartthrobs ("Celadus, the girls' heartthrob").
- **Diet:** gladiators were "barley-men" (*hordearii*, Pliny *NH* 18.72) who took a plant-ash tonic (*NH* 36.203). The Ephesus gladiator cemetery (2nd–3rd c.) confirms a largely vegetarian diet and healed wounds.
- **Female gladiators:** attested under Domitian, in Juvenal 6 and on the **Halicarnassus relief** ("Amazon" and "Achillia", who received *missio*). **Banned only in 200 by Septimius Severus.** Rare and spectacular.

### 10.3 Ranks, rules and the arena day

- **Ranks [A/P]:**
  - ***tiro***: novice, first fight;
  - ***veteranus***: has fought;
  - the barracks hierarchy of ***palus*** ranks (*primus palus*, the highest, then *secundus palus*...), mostly attested in the 2nd–3rd century [P];
  - ***doctor***/***magister***: trainer, often of a particular type;
  - ***rudiarius***: a gladiator freed with the **wooden sword (*rudis*)**, who may come back to fight for high fees or work as a trainer or referee.
- **Officials [A].** The *summa rudis* (chief referee) and *secunda rudis* (assistant) carry staffs to separate fighters and enforce rules. Attendants push reluctant fighters with whips and hot irons [P].
- **Missio and death [A]:**
  - A beaten fighter surrenders by **raising a finger** (*ad digitum*) and dropping his shield.
  - The **editor** (the sponsor; in Rome usually the emperor) decides, swayed by the crowd shouting "*Mitte!*" ("let him go!") or "*Iugula!*" ("cut his throat!").
  - "*Habet, hoc habet!*" means "he's had it!"
  - The **thumb gesture** (*pollice verso*, "with turned thumb") is real Latin, but **which direction meant death is unknown**. Avoid the modern thumbs-down cliché, or make the gesture ambiguous [A].
  - A fight could end ***stans missus***, a draw with both left standing.
  - Fights to the death without the possibility of reprieve (*sine missione*) were banned by Augustus [A].
  - Pompeian graffiti record bouts as **V** (*vicit*, won), **M** (*missus*, spared) and **Θ**/**P** (*periit*, died) [A].
  - **Death rate [P/U]:** most bouts did **not** end in death. Estimates for the 1st century suggest perhaps 1 in 5–10 fights killed a man; it rose in the later empire.
- **Arena day [A/P]:**
  1. ***Pompa***: procession of the editor, the fighters and the equipment.
  2. ***Probatio armorum***: weapons checked for sharpness.
  3. **Morning: *venationes*** (beast hunts).
  4. **Midday: executions** of condemned criminals (*noxii*), the *meridiani* (Seneca *Ep.* 7 is horrified).
  5. **Afternoon: gladiator pairs** to music (tuba, horns, water-organ).

  Corpses go out through the **Gate of Libitina** (the death goddess) to the *Spoliarium*. Attendants dressed as underworld gods who finished off and dragged the dead are described later (Tertullian, c. 200) [U for 113].
- **Name of the venue [A].** In 113 the Colosseum is simply ***amphitheatrum*** ("the amphitheatre"). The name "Colosseum" is medieval, taken from the nearby **Colossus** (Nero's statue, by now re-dedicated to Sol).

### 10.4 The Dacian triumph games of 107 and Trajan's other munera

- **Dio 68.15 [A].** Trajan gave **spectacles on 123 days**, in which **about 11,000 animals**, wild and tame, were killed and **10,000 gladiators** fought. Envoys from **India** attended.
- **Fasti Ostienses [A].** A fragment covering 108 to April 113 records another imperial ***munus* of 117 days with 4,941 pairs** of gladiators (some readings add an odd half-pair) in 108–109, alongside other shows. In-world, the city is **saturated with gladiatorial culture**, and many survivors, veterans, *rudiarii* and Dacian ex-prisoners are around in 113.
- **Naumachia [A].** Trajan dedicated his **Naumachia** (sea-battle basin) in the Vatican plain in **109**.

### 10.5 Venationes and executions

- **Beasts [A]:**
  - Lions, leopards (*Africanae*/*pantherae*), bears (including "Caledonian" bears, Mart. *Spect.*), bulls, **aurochs** (*uri*), **bison**, wild boar, deer, wolves, ostriches, gazelles, wild asses.
  - Rarely: **elephants** (imperial property), **rhinoceros** (on Domitian's coins), hippopotamus, crocodiles, tigers, giraffes (*camelopardales*), lynx.
  - The ***venatores*** (skilled hunters with spears, often "star" fighters) and ***bestiarii*** (lower-status beast-fighters) train at the **Ludus Matutinus**.
- **Executions [A]:**
  - ***Damnatio ad bestias***: exposure to beasts, sometimes tied to a stake on a cart (Zliten mosaic, Libya, 1st–2nd c.).
  - **Crucifixion**; burning in a pitch-soaked tunic (*tunica molesta*).
  - **Mythological re-enactments**: Martial's *Book of Spectacles* (AD 80) describes a criminal playing **Laureolus** (the bandit of a famous mime) really crucified and torn by a Caledonian bear; others as **Orpheus** killed by a bear, **Daedalus** and **Pasiphae**.
  - Ignatius of Antioch's "let me be food for the beasts" (*Letter to the Romans*) is traditionally set in this reign [U on the date].
- **Game note [G].** Execution content should be **implied, not shown in gameplay**: heard from the stands, discussed, or the subject of a rescue quest. That keeps a teen rating possible and the tone serious.

## 11. Anachronism watch-list for writers (society and people)

| Do not use in 113 | Why | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Colosseum" | The name is medieval | *Amphitheatrum*, "the amphitheatre" |
| *Panem et circenses*, *mens sana in corpore sano*, *quis custodiet ipsos custodes* as famous quotes | Juvenal Books 2 and 4 are later | Satire 3 material; Martial; Pliny |
| Free **bread** dole, pork, oil handouts | Severan and Aurelianic | Grain tokens at the Porticus Minucia |
| Diocles as star charioteer | Debut 122 | Invented stars |
| German imperial bodyguard | Disbanded in 69 | *Equites singulares*, praetorian *speculatores* |
| Legion at Albanum | Severan | No legions in Italy |
| Mithraeum of Santa Prisca, Circus Maximus mithraeum | Later | A small secret cult in a private house or cellar [G] |
| Serapeum on the Quirinal | Caracallan | The Iseum Campense |
| Christian catacombs, papal pomp | Later | House-church meetings; burial in ordinary cemeteries |
| *Taurobolium*, high priest of the Galli | Later 2nd c. | The Megalesia procession of the Galli |
| Hadrian as emperor, beards as elite fashion | After 117 | Clean-shaven elite |
| Formal *honestiores*/*humiliores* legal labels | Later jurists | De-facto status privilege; citizens versus slaves |
| Vigiles guardhouse of Cohort VII with graffiti | 215–245 | An invented sub-station |
| Popular elections and election graffiti in Rome | Elections moved to the Senate under Tiberius | Games notices, lost-property notices, faction cheers |
| Capitoline Games in 113 | They fall in 114 | Ludi Romani, the birthday games |
| "Optimus" as an official title | 114 | *Optimus princeps* used informally |
| Trajan's ashes in the Column | After 117 | The Column as a new monument (its base was intended as a tomb [P]) |
